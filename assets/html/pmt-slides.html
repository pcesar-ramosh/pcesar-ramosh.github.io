Proxy Means Test:
Targeting Poor Households in Bolivia

How we built a first household classification model with the 2023 Household Survey: assumptions, design, variables, targeting errors and what we were advised to improve.

Cesar Ramos

Based on joint work by a government technical team, 2025. Use the arrow keys or the buttons below. Charts are interactive.

Motivation

Why a proxy means test?

A social registry needs a rule to rank households by need when income cannot be verified.

Income is not observable

Most workers are informal: there are no payslips or tax records to check what a household declares.

Transfers are not targeted

The main cash transfers are universal or categorical (the elderly, mothers, students). Many beneficiaries are not poor.

Subsidies are costly

Replacing general subsidies with compensation for vulnerable households requires knowing who they are.

The idea goes back to Grosh and Baker (1995): use a few easy-to-observe characteristics as a "proxy" for means.

Design

The assumptions behind our PMT

Every PMT rests on choices like these. Making them explicit shows where errors can come from.

1 · Welfare is monthly income per capita

Household labor and non-labor income divided by the number of members. Consumption or multidimensional poverty are alternatives.

2 · State transfers are excluded

We want to know who is poor before the programs we are trying to target, to avoid circularity.

3 · Household = relatives sharing a budget

Head, spouse, children and other relatives. Domestic workers and non-relatives are left out.

4 · Proxies are observable and stable

Demographics, housing and durable goods: hard to hide, slow to change, and verifiable by an interviewer.

5 · Urban and rural areas differ

The same roof or fuel means different things in each area, so each gets its own model and variables.

6 · The survey relationship holds in the registry

Coefficients estimated on the sample are applied to all households. This is the strongest assumption.

Design

The model and the validation strategy

ln yh,a = β1′ demographicsh + β2′ housingh + β3′ assetsh + δregion + εh,a

Estimation

  • Weighted least squares with survey weights
  • One regression per area (a = urban, rural)
  • Household heads only: one row per household

Variable selection

  • Forward stepwise: a variable enters if p < 0.20
  • Then region fixed effects are added
  • Goal is prediction, not causal interpretation

Validation

  • Random split: 80% training, 20% test
  • All errors are measured on the test set

Variables

Three dimensions of observable proxies

Candidates were adapted to each area; stepwise selection kept 22 in the urban model and 31 in the rural one.

Demographics

  • Age composition (0–6, 7–11, 12–17, 18–60)
  • Dependency ratio, share of women
  • Head: sex, age, schooling, marital status
  • Highest schooling in the household
  • Member in the public sector
  • Remittances from abroad

Housing

  • Rooms per person
  • Dwelling type and tenure
  • Wall, floor and roof materials
  • Water source, toilet type
  • Electricity, cooking fuel
  • Garbage collection, internet

Durable goods

  • Refrigerator
  • Air conditioner
  • Washing machine
  • Car

RulePrefer items an interviewer can see and that households cannot easily hide or change.

Categories are grouped differently by area: for example, wooden walls are a separate category only in the rural model, and broadband (not mobile) internet is used in the urban one.

Classification

From predicted income to eligibility: two errors

  • Rank test households by predicted income (percentiles 1–100).
  • Baseline rule: a household is eligible if its percentile is below the poverty rate, so coverage equals the number of poor.
  • Compare with actual poverty status in the survey.
Exclusion error = poor not selected / all poor
Inclusion error = non-poor selected / all selected

Exclusion is undercoverage (1 − sensitivity). Inclusion is leakage. With coverage fixed at the poverty rate, both move together; changing the cutoff trades one for the other.

Which error matters more?

For a safety net, leaving out a poor household is usually the costlier mistake. Inclusion errors cost budget; exclusion errors cost welfare.

Three facts

  • Errors are lower in rural areas: poverty is more common there and housing variables predict better.
  • Methods that push exclusion down (class weights, over-sampling) push inclusion up.
  • No method dominates: the choice depends on which error the program wants to avoid.

Why exclusion errors are high

The model compresses the distribution

A regression predicts the average, so the poorest look richer than they are and the richest look poorer.

Explained variation

R² is about 0.41 (urban) and 0.46 (rural) in training. More than half of the variation in income is not captured by the proxies.

Consequence

Poor households with "non-poor looking" characteristics are predicted above the cutoff. This is the known weak point of PMTs (Brown, Ravallion and van de Walle, 2018).

Design

A 0–100 score instead of a single line

scoreh = 100 × ( ŷh − ŷmin ) / ( ŷmax − ŷmin )
  • 0 is the poorest predicted household, 100 the richest.
  • Each program can set its own cutoff on the same score: a narrow one for a small budget, a wide one for a subsidy compensation.
  • The cutoff can be chosen to balance or minimize errors instead of copying the official poverty line.

The scoring approach follows the design used for a conditional cash transfer in Honduras (Benedetti, Farach, Ibarrarán and Villa, 2014).

External review

What we were advised to improve

Feedback from a multilateral development bank that reviewed the roadmap for the social registry.

1 · Decide the metric and the objective first

Income, consumption, wealth or multidimensional poverty? And should the algorithm minimize targeting errors or maximize predictive fit? These are different models.

2 · Use only relevant and verifiable variables

Re-estimate with the latest survey and keep predictors that the registry form can collect and check.

3 · Cross-check with administrative records

Tax, property and vehicle registries reveal assets and reduce leakage. This needs interoperability agreements, not only software.

4 · Add geography

Build a poverty map from the 2024 census with small-area estimation, and use geographic or administrative filters to exclude the clearly non-vulnerable.

5 · Pilot, then recalibrate

Test the questionnaire and processes in the field; recalibrate the model with the data the registry itself collects.

6 · Build quality control around the model

Automatic validations, re-interviews and audits; protocols to update, rectify and appeal; a validity period for each household's data.

Wrap-up

Key points and next steps

What we learned

  • A simple regression with about 25 observable variables explains roughly 40–45% of income variation.
  • At the poverty-rate cutoff, the model misses 4 to 5 in 10 poor urban households and about 3 in 10 rural ones.
  • Exclusion and inclusion errors trade off; machine learning shifts the balance more than it removes it.
  • Results move a few points with the random split, so comparisons need repeated splits.

Next steps

  • Agree on the welfare metric and the optimization criterion.
  • Re-estimate with the newest survey and verifiable variables; test quantile and poverty-weighted models.
  • Use cross-validation instead of a single split.
  • Poverty map with the 2024 census.
  • Pilot the registry form and recalibrate.
